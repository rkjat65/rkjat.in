U.P.'s ₹59,327 Crore 'Revenue Surplus': Prudence or Underspending?

A surplus built by not delivering budgeted programmes is not the same achievement as a surplus built by stronger tax capacity and efficient execution.

Radhakishan Jat/9 July 2026/14 min read
IndiaEconomyPolicyData VizFiscal FederalismUttar Pradesh
ShareXLinkedIn

In late June 2026, Uttar Pradesh Chief Minister Yogi Adityanath told industry leaders in Bengaluru that the State has been “revenue surplus for the last six years” — proof, in the political narrative, that U.P. is shedding its old BIMARU image.

The Comptroller and Auditor General’s 2024-25 State Finances reporting lends the number real weight: U.P. not only posted a revenue surplus, it posted the largest one — ₹59,327 crore — nearly half of the ₹1.27 lakh crore combined surplus of the 13 surplus states.

That headline is true. The interpretation often attached to it is incomplete.

This data story asks a harder fiscal question: is the surplus evidence of prudence, or of a developing state that still cannot spend its own budget?

Read this first

A revenue surplus means revenue receipts exceeded revenue expenditure. It is not the same as a fiscal surplus, and it says nothing automatic about development outcomes. The charts below use CAG / RBI / Finance Accounts-style figures as compiled in public analysis for FY 2024-25. This is public-finance storytelling, not a party brief.

What a revenue surplus actually measures

Before the charts, fix the definition:

Revenue surplus = total revenue receipts − total revenue expenditure

It does not include capital expenditure. A state can report a large revenue surplus while still:

  • running a fiscal deficit (borrowing for capital and other needs),
  • underspending capital budgets,
  • leaving centrally sponsored scheme funds idle, and
  • missing service-delivery targets that depend on timely spending.

So the surplus can be a healthy sign of tax buoyancy and expenditure control — or it can be an accounting residue of weak absorption capacity. The data for U.P. point strongly toward the second reading.

Key takeaway

In 2024-25, U.P.’s revenue realisation was about 16% short of budget — yet it still reported a record surplus because revenue spending fell short by even more (~15.4%).

The arithmetic of the surplus

Start with what the State planned versus what it delivered.

Budgeted receipts: ₹6,06,802 crore. Actual: ₹5,10,213 crore. That is a large shortfall on the revenue side — not a windfall year relative to the budget speech.

Then look at spending:

Budgeted revenue expenditure: ₹5,32,655 crore. Actual: ₹4,50,886 crore. Underspend on the revenue account alone is in the order of ₹82,000 crore.

Put simply:

  • Receipts came in below the budget.
  • Spending came in even further below the budget.
  • The gap between actual receipts and actual revenue spend is the celebrated ₹59,327 crore surplus.

That is mathematically a surplus. It is not the same story as “we collected more than we promised and still funded everything we promised.”

Maharashtra: similar scale, opposite fiscal posture

To see how unusual this is, compare U.P. with Maharashtra — another large economy with a comparable order of magnitude in state finances.

Maharashtra nearly met its revenue targets and spent more of what it budgeted. Result: a revenue deficit of about ₹29,995 crore.

Revenue balance +₹59,327 Cr surplus −₹29,995 Cr deficit

Neither outcome is automatically “good” or “bad.” A deficit can mean investment ambition or weak revenue. A surplus can mean discipline or delivery failure. The comparison forces the right question: are we celebrating balance-sheet aesthetics or budget execution?

Counterfactuals: what if U.P. had spent like its peers?

The cleanest way to test the “prudence” claim is a set of counterfactuals: hold U.P.’s receipts roughly fixed and ask what the revenue balance would look like if the State had spent at different rates.

Reading the bars left to right:

  1. Actual (−15.35% underspend on budgeted revex): +₹59,327 crore surplus
  2. If spent as budgeted: −₹22,442 crore deficit
  3. If spent at Maharashtra’s rate (−1.47%): −₹14,612 crore deficit
  4. If spent at Madhya Pradesh’s rate (−4.86%): +₹3,445 crore surplus
  5. If spent at the all-states average (−6.09%): +₹9,996 crore surplus

Key takeaway

The record surplus is not robust to normal spending. At full budgeted execution — or even Maharashtra-like utilisation — U.P. would have reported a revenue deficit, not a trophy surplus.

That is the analytical heart of the story. Underspending is doing the heavy lifting.

This is not a one-year quirk

CAG and RBI series show U.P. has consistently underspent budgeted expenditure by at least about 15% every year since 2019-20. The heatmap pattern in the public Data Point analysis is unambiguous: among large-GSDP states, U.P. sits in the deep underspend band for most of the last decade, especially after 2018-19.

A single year of underspend can be COVID friction, administrative lag, or election timing. A decade-long pattern is a capacity and planning problem:

  • Over-optimistic budget estimates (receipts and outlays)
  • Slow project clearances and fund release pipelines
  • Weak last-mile absorption in departments and districts
  • Political preference for headline fiscal optics over full utilisation

PRS has flagged this pattern for U.P. in earlier years as well: under-spending can make fiscal deficit look better than the budget promised — not because programmes were delivered cheaper, but because they were not delivered at scale.

Capital expenditure: the deeper wound

Revenue underspend is already large. Capital underspend is worse.

  • Revenue expenditure: about −15.4% vs budget
  • Capital expenditure: about −27% vs budget

In absolute terms, the State spent only about ₹1.13 lakh crore of a ₹1.55 lakh crore capital outlay — a gap near 27%.

For a state still building roads, power, irrigation, urban infrastructure, and health facilities, capital underspend is not thrift. It is delayed capability. Celebrating a revenue surplus while leaving capex money on the table is like applauding money left in a household bank account because the roof was never repaired.

Why this distinction matters

FRBM-style targets reward revenue balance. Voters experience schools, hospitals, and jobs. A developing state can “win” on FRBM optics while losing on physical capital formation if it systematically underspends capex.

Central money: dependence plus unspent funds

U.P.’s revenue stack is also more transfer-dependent than industrial peers.

U.P. sits near 55% of total revenue from Central transfers. A peer cluster of more industrialised large states (Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Gujarat, Telangana) sits closer to the mid-20s. Another large-state cluster (Madhya Pradesh, West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Rajasthan) sits in the high 40s.

The sharp dip around 2019-20 is not a structural miracle of own-revenue. It was driven by a one-time “book transfer” of ₹71,180 crore from the Sinking Fund into non-tax revenue — a CAG-flagged accounting episode that inflated non-tax receipts and distorted the transfer-share ratio for that year. Without that transfer, the Central share would have remained near the mid-50s.

Even budgeted Central scheme funds go unspent

The State receives the highest allocation among peers for many Central schemes — and still underspends the GoI share aggressively.

  • Budgeted: ₹99,197 crore
  • Expenditure: ₹32,641 crore
  • Shortfall: −38.4%

U.P. is not merely a high-transfer state. It is a high-transfer, low-absorption state on this measure. That combination should humble any pure surplus celebration.

So is the surplus fake?

No. The surplus is real in accounting terms. Revenue receipts exceeded revenue expenditure by ₹59,327 crore. CAG’s multi-state compilation places U.P. at the top of the surplus list for 2024-25.

What is oversold is the political meaning:

Claim often implied What the data show
“We earn more than we spend” True for revenue account; fiscal deficit can still exist
“Proof of fiscal turnaround” Partly true on balance; weak on execution fidelity
“BIMARU no more via surplus alone” Developmental status needs growth, jobs, health, education, infrastructure — not only FRBM optics
“Highest surplus = best managed state” Highest surplus partly means least full utilisation among large budgets

A fairer summary:

Uttar Pradesh has improved its revenue-account optics and can claim a large surplus. But the same data show chronic underspending — especially on capital and Central schemes — that would erase most or all of that surplus under peer-like utilisation. The surplus is real. The hype is incomplete.

Key takeaway

Treat U.P.’s surplus as a dual signal: some fiscal consolidation capacity, and a serious absorption/execution gap. Reporting only the first number is incomplete journalism.

What good fiscal storytelling should watch next

For readers, students, and policymakers, better questions than “is U.P. surplus or deficit?” include:

  1. Budget credibility: How close are actual receipts and outlays to BE and RE each year?
  2. Capex conversion: Of budgeted capital outlay, what share becomes assets on the ground?
  3. Scheme absorption: For Centrally Sponsored Schemes, what share of GoI + State share is actually spent?
  4. Own-revenue depth: Is the transfer share falling because own taxes are rising — or only because of accounting one-offs?
  5. Peer-adjusted surplus: What would the revenue balance be at all-states or peer utilisation rates?

Those questions turn a press-release number into a development diagnostic.

Three structural lessons

1. Fiscal optics and development delivery can diverge

FRBM targets and political speeches reward revenue balance. Households experience roads, hospitals, and jobs. A state can look “disciplined” while systematically failing to spend capital and scheme funds.

2. Large budgets without absorption capacity create phantom ambition

U.P. budgets at a scale befitting India’s most populous state. The underspend pattern suggests the binding constraint is often implementation bandwidth, not only resource scarcity.

3. Counterfactuals beat single-number triumphs

The most powerful chart in this story is not the surplus bar. It is the counterfactual panel: spend like the budget, or like Maharashtra, and the surplus largely disappears. That is how data storytelling should test claims.

Methodology

Primary framing sources: CAG State Finances reporting for 2024-25 (multi-state surplus/deficit tallies), State Finance and Appropriation Accounts submitted to CAG, RBI State Finances publications, and PRS Legislative Research budget notes for Uttar Pradesh.

Display dataset: CSV — budgeted vs actual receipts and revenue expenditure for U.P. and Maharashtra; counterfactual surplus outcomes; revenue vs capital underspend; Central transfer share; GoI central-scheme budget vs spend.

Key definitions:

  • Revenue surplus = revenue receipts − revenue expenditure (excludes capital expenditure).
  • Underspend % = (actual − budgeted) / budgeted × 100 for the relevant expenditure head.
  • Counterfactuals re-estimate U.P.’s revenue balance at alternative underspend rates while holding the story’s published receipt context fixed as in the public Data Point analysis.

Caveats:

  • Some multi-year series (exact annual underspend heatmaps, peer group averages) are illustrative reconstructions aligned to published endpoints and qualitative CAG/RBI patterns, not a full scrape of every Finance Account cell.
  • 2019-20 Central-transfer share is distorted by a one-time book transfer of ₹71,180 crore from the Sinking Fund.
  • Revenue surplus should not be confused with fiscal surplus or with development outcomes.
  • This story is independent analysis for public understanding; it is not affiliated with any government or party.

Bottom line

Uttar Pradesh’s ₹59,327 crore revenue surplus is a legitimate CAG-era headline. It is also a stress test for how we read state finances.

If the State had spent as budgeted — or even closer to peer utilisation — the surplus would shrink sharply or flip into deficit. Capital and Central scheme underspends make the gap look less like thrift and more like delivery shortfall.

Prudence is real when a state collects better, prioritises better, and still funds what it promised. Leaving the budget unspent is not the same virtue. The data invite a more honest scoreboard: not only did U.P. report a surplus? but did U.P. spend the development money it put on paper?
